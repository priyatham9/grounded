Grounded · proposed endeavour

Trustworthy, source-grounded AI and data quality for workplace and process safety.

This page states what I propose to keep doing in the United States over the next three years, what already exists to build on, and how progress can be checked. The plan sections describe intentions. They are not results, and nothing here promises an outcome.

What already exists The three-year plan

The endeavour

The proposed endeavour is to make the numbers and the AI answers that workplace and process safety decisions rest on easier to check against their sources. It has two parts: methods and open data that test how reliable public injury and chemical-release records are when rates and rankings are computed from them, and a public benchmark that tests whether AI answers about safety regulations are grounded in the correct clause for the correct equipment.

All work is released as open code, data and tests under the MIT licence, so that federal agencies, researchers and employers can rerun it on the same public files.

Why it matters nationally

Private-industry employers reported 2.5 million nonfatal injuries and illnesses in 2024 [4], and 5,070 workers died from work injuries that year [11]. OSHA cites almost $1 billion per week in direct workers' compensation costs [10]. Employers with 250 or more employees, and those with 20 to 249 in designated industries, must submit injury summary data to OSHA each year [1], and OSHA publishes it [2].

GAO found that employers reported no summary injury data for more than half of the establishments it estimated were required to report [12], and in 2024 that the extent of underreporting is unknown on both OSHA recordkeeping forms and BLS surveys [14]. EPA's Risk Management Program requires covered chemical facilities to file a five-year accident history [5][6]. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework lists "valid and reliable" as a necessary condition of trustworthy AI [8].

This programme addresses a related gap: errors inside records that are submitted, and AI answers that read as correct but cite the wrong rule. The full mapping from each repository to a federal interest is in the national importance brief, which also holds the complete source list. Numbers in brackets refer to that list; the sources used here are repeated below.

The three-year plan

These are plans, not results. Each milestone is something that can be checked by looking at a repository or a public record. A milestone can slip or fail, and failure to meet one will be stated here rather than removed.

Year 1 · plan

Evaluate and publish

  • Run language models on the 68-item grounding benchmark with the existing adapter, in ungrounded and grounded arms, when API access allows, and commit the run files and a results table.
  • Publish the OSHA paper and the grounding benchmark paper as public preprints, and submit each to a peer-reviewed venue.
  • Mint DOIs for the code and data releases.
  • Finish the pending page-range checks on the bundled paper's references.
Year 2 · plan

Widen and strengthen

  • Add more minimal pairs to the benchmark, each sourced to a CFR section, and report the baselines again on the larger set.
  • Extend the ontology work to the chemical plant data, so that plant, process, chemical and accident records are linked objects.
  • Add the state-level and industry-level views of filing quality that are planned but not yet merged.
  • Respond to reviewer comments on submitted papers and record each outcome in the changelog.
Year 3 · plan

Make it usable by others

  • Package the benchmark runner as a documented workflow that another team can run on its own system, with a self-test that needs no keys.
  • Publish the TRIR and DART library and the filing checker as versioned releases with release notes.
  • Repeat the language-model evaluation with whichever models are current and report the change since Year 1.
  • Write a short public summary of what the programme found, what did not hold up, and what remains open.

How success will be measured

The programme tracks the following counts in a dated log. Each is a public, checkable number.

  • DOIs minted, papers posted, papers submitted, papers accepted.
  • Citations to the papers and datasets (Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar).
  • External stars, forks, issues and pull requests; package downloads; marketplace installs.
  • Expert contacts made and replies received.
  • Reviewer invitations, talks accepted, press mentions.

The baseline on 27 October 2026 is zero for every one of these. Counts will be reported as they are, including when they stay low.

Risks and limits

  • No language model has been evaluated on the benchmark. Whether models show the substitution failure the benchmark is built to expose is unknown until that run is done, and the result may be that they do not.
  • The benchmark has 68 items. Its baselines show the items discriminate between simple methods, not that it measures every kind of grounding.
  • Model API access, cost and changing model versions can delay or alter the evaluation.
  • The injury and chemical-release findings are correlations in reported data. They do not identify which specific numbers are wrong and do not explain causes.
  • The risk-modelling work uses simulated data only.
  • No paper has had external peer review. Reviewers may find problems that change or withdraw a claim.
  • The work is released openly. No adoption by any agency, company or researcher is claimed or assumed.
  • Sole-author work limits throughput. The plan is sized to what one person can complete and may shrink.
