A rupture disk question came back with relief valve parameters, and it read as competent

Two parameter spaces that do not intersect, and nothing in the wording signals the swap.

01

Asked about a rupture disk, answered about a relief valve

Fluent, well formatted, and inapplicable to the device in front of the engineer. The two devices share no parameters, so every value in the answer is plausible for the device it was not about.

Why the substitution survives review

Nothing in the answer is wrong on its own terms

The two parameter spaces do not intersect, so every value is a plausible value for a relief valve. A reviewer checking internal consistency finds none of the usual tells: no contradictions, no missing units, no hedging. The error lives in the binding between the question and the device, and that binding was never made.

Authoritative binding replaces free retrieval: every claim carries a source clause and an anchor span. A benchmark scores abstention as a correct response when the corpus does not contain the answer. The ontology makes the device and context explicit before any parameter is named. And the data work underneath asks whether the public benchmarks used to check any of this are themselves trustworthy.

Why it recurs: four mechanisms converge
  1. 1

    No retrieval happened

    The answer came from parametric memory, not a bound source.

  2. 2

    Correct content is long tail

    Relief valves dominate the training distribution. Disks do not.

  3. 3

    Abstention is penalised

    A confident answer scores better than "not in my sources".

  4. 4

    The corpus is lexically adversarial

    Standards share vocabulary across devices that share no parameters.

What it means for a leader

An AI answer can be fluent, internally consistent and about the wrong device. A review that checks tone and consistency will pass it, because the error sits in the link between the question and the source.

Ask your team

“For this answer, which clause did it come from, and does that clause govern our device or its neighbour?”

06

Six questions to take into Monday's safety review

One per project. Each takes under an hour and needs nothing from this programme except the question.

  1. Screen the hours before you quote the rate.Divide hours worked by average employees for the rows behind your last rate. Anything outside 120 to 4,500 a year is a filing you cannot compute a rate from. On the public file those rows are 2.07% of filings and 96.68% of hours.The denominator →
  2. Ask for the clause, not the answer.Open the cited clause behind the last five AI-assisted answers your team acted on, and check it governs the thing in the question, not its neighbour. An answer with no clause has not been checked.The adjacent clause →
  3. Ask which condition, not who.Re-read your last three serious investigations. Where a conclusion is a label about a person, name the conditions instead: procedure, familiarity, a window fixed downstream. Each has a record on site.Why people err →
  4. Ask for the n and the interval method.For the coefficient that drove the last risk-model decision, get the sample size and ask for a bootstrap interval. In these simulations a usable path needs 369 units and ranking two paths needs 6,884.Where the method breaks →
  5. Take both paybacks to the committee.Run the next rejected capital request twice: booked lines only, then with lost time, retraining, productivity and morale priced and marked as estimates. State the assumed risk reduction in the same sentence.The capital case →
  6. Fix the peer group before the rate.Write down each site's six-digit NAICS code and size band before anyone computes a percentile, and put the n beside every rank. Any group under about 50 establishments gets a caveat, not a headline.Compared with whom →
